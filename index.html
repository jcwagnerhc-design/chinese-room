Inside the Chinese Room
    A thought experiment by John Searle, 1980

    Inside the Chinese Room

    You are locked in a room. Slips of paper covered in symbols come through a slot in the door. You can't read them. You have a rulebook that says which symbols to send back. Your shift is four slips long.

    Searle imagined Chinese because he couldn't read a word of it. Some of you can, so this room uses Tessel, a script made up for this game. Treat it as a real language you have never seen.

    The slot goes dark. Four slips in, four replies out, every shape correct. Then you hear the lock turn.

    The door opens

    Here is what you said.

    The person outside was a student. Tessel is their first language. This is the conversation they just had with the room.

    They walked away sure that someone in the room had listened and understood. You were matching shapes.

    Out of the room

    The room is a thought experiment. These four cases are happening now, and each one turns on the question you just answered seven times.

    Your case file
    For the room you're sitting in
    1. Searle sat you in the operator's chair. How would the experiment feel if you had played the rulebook, or the student outside?
    2. Which rebuild moved the most people in this class? What does that version have that the original room lacks?
    3. Name a test for understanding that a person can pass and the room can't. Could a bigger rulebook pass it?
    4. If we can never settle whether a system understands, what should the rule be for how it talks to people?
    5. Your neurons follow chemistry as blindly as you followed the rulebook. Is your brain a room? If it isn't, what is the difference?
    Teacher notes
    • About 20 minutes solo. Students can copy their case file at the end and paste it wherever you collect work. Progress is saved on each student's own device and nowhere else.
    • To run it from the projector, unlock every section and take verdicts by show of hands.
    • The six rebuilds follow the standard replies: systems, scale and speed (the Churchlands' luminous room), robot (Harnad's symbol grounding), learning from text (Bender and Koller's octopus), brain simulator, and gradual neuron replacement (Searle 1992, Chalmers 1996).
    • Searle's positions are paraphrased from “Minds, Brains, and Programs” (Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 1980) and The Rediscovery of the Mind (1992). No text here is a direct quotation.
